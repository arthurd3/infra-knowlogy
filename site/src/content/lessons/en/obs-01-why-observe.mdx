This track has a peculiarity: **the code it explains has been running since the
first module.** `make obs` brings up Prometheus, Grafana, Loki, Alloy and four
exporters — hardened, resource-capped, with the Docker socket behind an
allowlist proxy (ADR 0003). What was missing was the lesson.

## Monitoring and observing are not synonyms

The distinction repeated everywhere — "monitoring is *known unknowns*,
observability is *unknown unknowns*" — is true and nearly useless in practice,
because it does not tell you what to do.

The useful version is operational: **monitoring is having the answer ready to a
question you already asked; observing is being able to ask a new question
without touching the code.** A CPU dashboard is monitoring. Discovering, during
an incident, that the problem only affects requests that went through the cache
is observability — and it is only possible if the cache label is already in the
data.

<Term word="cardinality" alt="cardinality" open>
How many distinct label combinations a metric can have.

`api_http_requests_total{method, route, status}` with 4 methods, 6 routes and 5
statuses has a maximum cardinality of 4 × 6 × 5 = 120 series. Adding a `user_id`
label multiplies that by the number of users — and that is how a monitoring
system goes down.

It is the price of the new question: every label you store today is a question
you can ask tomorrow, and a cost you pay continuously. Lesson 4 measures it in
this stack.
</Term>

## The three signals, and what each refuses to do

<SignalsAndQuestions />

What the figure shows and almost no "three pillars" text says: they are neither
interchangeable nor hierarchical. They are **expensive along different
dimensions**.

<Callout type="why" label="Why a metric is cheap and a log is not">
A metric is one number per series per scrape. A thousand requests in fifteen
seconds become **one** counter increment — the cost does not grow with traffic,
it grows with **cardinality**.

A log is one line per event. A thousand requests become a thousand lines. The
cost grows linearly with traffic, which is why everybody ends up sampling logs
and nobody samples metrics.
</Callout>

## What this stack has, measured

<RunIt>
```bash
make obs
curl -s http://127.0.0.1:9090/api/v1/targets?state=active \
  | python3 -c 'import sys,json; d=json.load(sys.stdin)["data"]["activeTargets"]; print(len(d), "targets,", sum(1 for t in d if t["health"]=="up"), "up")'
```
</RunIt>

Seven targets: the Go api and the Python worker, instrumented in the
application; cAdvisor reading cgroups per container; node-exporter reading the
host; the Postgres and Redis exporters; and Prometheus itself.

`site/src/data/obs-measured.json` carries this machine's numbers, rewritten by
`make verify`: active series, Prometheus resident memory, and how much each
target publishes before and after filtering.

<Callout type="trap" label="Tracing is the signal this stack does NOT have">
And the lesson will not pretend otherwise. Distributed tracing requires
propagating a context identifier along the whole chain — api, Redis, worker —
and none of the three applications does that today.

The path would be OpenTelemetry, which became the standard after OpenTracing
merged with OpenCensus in 2019. It is declared as a gap in the roadmap, not
hidden behind a "three pillars" diagram that implies completeness.
</Callout>

## The fourth question

Metrics, logs and traces together still do not answer: **is this bad enough to
wake somebody up?**

That is not a technical question. It depends on an agreement about how much
failure is acceptable — which is what SLIs, SLOs and error budgets are for, and
they are lessons 6 and 7 of this track. Without them, what remains is somebody
staring at a dashboard and deciding under pressure.

<FieldNote
  source="Google SRE Workbook — Implementing SLOs"
  url="https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/"
  kind="book"
  accessed="2026-09-20"
>
The book is explicit about this: alerting on symptoms (high CPU, full disk)
produces pages nobody knows what to do with, because a symptom does not imply
impact. The alert worth having starts from **how much of the error budget is
being spent** — and that requires having defined the budget first.
</FieldNote>

<LabExercise check="todos os alvos do prometheus estão up" file="stack/services/observability/prometheus.yml">

`prometheus.yml` has seven `scrape_configs`. One of them scrapes
`localhost:9090`.

Question: why does Prometheus scrape itself, and what is lost without that?

<Fragment slot="hint">
Think about what happens when the problem is the monitoring itself.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

Because Prometheus is also a service that can be unwell — and if it does not
measure itself, the only sign that it is in trouble is the absence of signal,
which is precisely what nobody notices.

It publishes metrics about itself: how many series it holds in memory, how many
samples it ingests per second, how long each rule evaluation takes, how many
scrapes failed.

```bash
curl -s --get http://127.0.0.1:9090/api/v1/query \
  --data-urlencode 'query=prometheus_tsdb_head_series'
```

It is also what makes lesson 4 measurable: `scrape_samples_scraped` and
`scrape_samples_post_metric_relabeling` are metrics Prometheus keeps about its
own collection work, and they are what the gate uses to prove the cardinality
filter is cutting.

The honest limit: if Prometheus goes down entirely, it will not report its own
outage. What solves that is a second Prometheus scraping the first — a common
arrangement in production, and one this stack does not have.

</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- Monitoring is having the answer ready; observing is being able to ask the new
  question.
- The three signals answer **different** questions and cost along different
  dimensions: metrics grow with cardinality, logs grow with traffic.
- This stack has metrics and logs, and **has no traces** — that is declared.
- The fourth question, "does this deserve waking somebody?", is not technical,
  and it is the subject of lessons 6 and 7.
