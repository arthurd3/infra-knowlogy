Every other lesson in this track quotes a number that came out of a command on
this machine. **This one does not.**

Incident culture is not measurable on a laptop. What follows is **cited**, with
source and access date — and it is knowledge of a different nature, not of lesser
quality: it comes from decades of safety research into complex systems, done in
aviation and medicine before it reached software.

## The problem with "the root cause"

<CauseVsContext />

<Term word="Root cause" alt="root cause">
The idea that every incident has **one** cause, findable if you ask "why" enough
times.

The criticism is not philosophical. It is that the search **always ends in the
same place**: at the last person who touched the system. Not because they are
guilty, but because that is where the chain of "whys" stops having mechanical
answers and starts having human ones — and the investigation stops, satisfied.

The result is a corrective action that corrects nothing: "be more careful",
"review more thoroughly", "train the team". None of them changes a condition of
the system.
</Term>

Richard Cook formulated this in 1998, about operating theatres rather than
servers:

> Complex systems run in degraded mode. They are always carrying latent faults,
> because the system is too complex not to have defects. No single fault causes
> an accident; it takes **several** combining.

Translated to a stack: your system **already** has the defects of the next
incident. They are there right now, and nothing has fallen over yet because no
combination has closed.

## The question that replaces it

> **"Why did this make sense, to the person doing it, at that moment?"**

It is the central question of what Allspaw called a *just culture*, and it
changes what the investigation finds.

If the answer is "because the dashboard was green", you found a dashboard to fix.
If it is "because the procedure says to do it that way", you found a procedure.
If it is "because it was the third time that night", you found an on-call
problem.

None of those answers is "the person was careless" — and all of them are
actionable.

<Callout type="warn" title="“Blameless” is not “accountability-free”">
It is the commonest misunderstanding, and it discredits the whole practice.

A *just culture* does not say nobody answers for anything. It distinguishes
**error** from **deliberate violation**, and treats the two differently. What it
refuses is the shortcut of stopping the investigation once a name appears.

Allspaw's formulation is operational: the organisation gains more by letting
whoever made the mistake **describe in detail what happened** than by punishing.
Punishment does not remove the condition — it removes the account.
</Callout>

## What a useful write-up contains

<Tradeoff
  a="Postmortem as a record"
  b="Postmortem as learning"
  whenA="When there is an obligation to account to third parties — contract, audit, regulation. The document has to exist and be consistent, and that is a legitimate end."
  whenB="When the goal is for the next incident not to happen. There the value is in the process of writing, and a document nobody read afterwards still did half the work."
>
  <Fragment slot="a">
    Timeline, impact, duration, corrective action with an owner and a deadline.
    In exchange: it becomes ritual, and the form becomes the product.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="b">
    Adds what **surprised** the responders, what was hard to find out, and which
    hypotheses were discarded along the way. In exchange: it takes longer and
    needs somebody to facilitate.
  </Fragment>
</Tradeoff>

The section that teaches most and is almost always missing is **"what surprised
us"**. It records the distance between the operators' mental model and the real
system — and that distance is the same one that will cause the next incident.

## Where this touches this repository

The earlier lessons in this track are all examples of a wrong mental model found
by measurement:

- the host's `ps` calling Postgres `avahi`;
- the application reading 31 GiB and dying at 384 MiB;
- the load-average alert firing after the load ended;
- the mistyped service name costing eight seconds and looking like the network.

None of those surprises was found by somebody being careful. They were found by
somebody **measuring**. It is the engineering version of the same idea: the
system only tells you what you ask it.

<FieldNote
  source="Richard I. Cook, MD — How Complex Systems Fail (Revision G)"
  url="https://how.complexsystems.fail/"
  accessed="2026-09-21"
>
Cook's treatise has eighteen points and fits in four pages. Three of them change
how you read an incident: complex systems **run in degraded mode** all the time;
root-cause attribution is **fundamentally wrong** because there is no isolated
point of failure; and the work of the people at the sharp end simultaneously
produces safety **and** is the most visible source of failure — which guarantees
they are the easiest target. Written in 1998 about medicine, and cited in nearly
every serious SRE text since. Not measured here: it is systems-safety research,
not the state of this machine.
</FieldNote>

  <Fragment slot="hint">
    Not "why" again — that continues the chain in the same direction.

  <Fragment slot="answer">

> **"Why did that action make sense, to that person, at that moment?"**

It forces the investigation to describe **context** instead of continuing the
chain, and that is where the actionable conditions appear: what the dashboard
showed, what the procedure said, how long the person had been awake, how many
alerts they had already taken that night.

Two follow-ups that usually yield more than the rest of the meeting:

- **"What surprised you?"** — measures the distance between the mental model and
  the real system.
- **"What was hard to find out?"** — becomes an observability item, and is the
  one part of a postmortem that generates direct technical work.

And the final test for a corrective action: **would it be effective if the same
person did the same thing again tomorrow?** "Be more careful" fails that test.
"Rollback takes 20 minutes and needs to take 2" passes.

  </Fragment>
</LabExercise>
