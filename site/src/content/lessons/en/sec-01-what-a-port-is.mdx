There is a poster that circulates a lot, and it is the reason this track exists:
**"the most attacked ports"**, eight rows, number, service, use and "common
attacks". It is a good hook. It is also a table that only makes sense to someone
who already knows what a port is — and to someone who does not, it becomes a
list of numbers to memorise.

This lesson is the foundation. By the end of it you will be able to answer,
without looking anything up: what a port is, why an attacker can sweep the
entire internet, what he learns from each answer, and why changing your port
number is nearly useless.

## What a port is not

It is not a hole in your computer. It is not a place. It is not something you
open and close like a window.

<Term word="port" alt="porta" open>
A port is a **16-bit number** — 0 to 65,535 — that travels inside every network
packet, next to the address. The address says *which machine*; the port says
*which conversation inside that machine*.

The operating system keeps a table: "who asked to receive whatever arrives on
port 8080?". When a packet comes in, it reads the number, consults the table and
hands the data to the right process. If nobody claimed that number, there is
nobody to hand it to.

Which is why "port 80 is open" really means: **there is a process running that
asked the system to receive whatever arrives on port 80**. The port has no
existence of its own — it is a row in a table.
</Term>

The building analogy works as a start: the IP address is the building, the port
is the apartment number. But it breaks at one important point, and that point is
this whole track's subject: **in a building, apartment 402 exists even if nobody
lives there.** On a network, it does not. If no process is listening, there is
no apartment — only a number nobody claimed.

## The three answers, and the map they draw

When somebody sends a packet to one of your ports, the answer is one of three —
and the three say different things:

| What comes back | What it means | What the attacker concludes |
|---|---|---|
| `SYN-ACK` | a process accepted the connection | **there is a service here** |
| `RST` | the machine exists, nobody is listening | the machine is alive |
| nothing | a firewall swallowed the packet | **somebody is filtering** |

Notice that the third answer is the **absence** of an answer, and it informs
too. It is the difference between "nobody is home" and "somebody is home and
does not want to talk" — and that difference is exactly what separates closing a
port from filtering it.

<Term word="port scan" alt="varredura de portas">
Sending a "hello" packet to every port of an address and writing down what
answers. Sweeping all 65,535 ports of **one** address takes seconds.

And this is where most people's intuition fails: the attacker does not sweep
65,535 ports of one target. He does the opposite — he picks **one** port and
sweeps millions of addresses with it, which is cheaper still. Mass-scanning
tools can cover the entire IPv4 address space on a single port in a matter of
minutes, from one machine.

Which is why the question "who would want to attack me?" is badly formed. Nobody
picked you. You were one of the addresses on the list.
</Term>

## Why "most attacked port" is even a thing

If scanning happens per port rather than per target, the attacker has to choose
**which** port to sweep. And he chooses on the same logic as any business: the
one with the best chance of paying off per attempt.

That explains the poster's entire list. Port 23 (Telnet) is on it not because
Telnet is especially fragile today, but because millions of cheap devices still
run it with the factory password. Port 3389 (RDP) is on it because one hit is
worth an administrator session inside a corporate network. Port 445 (SMB) is on
it because it produced a worm once.

<FieldNote kind="blog" source="SANS Internet Storm Center" url="https://isc.sans.edu/data/trends.html" when="accessed 2026-09-19">
The ranking of most-scanned ports is **measured**, not opined: the Internet
Storm Center aggregates firewall logs from sensors worldwide and publishes which
ports take the most attempts, with today's numbers.

It is worth opening and comparing against the poster. Positions shift from week
to week — it is a live phenomenon that responds to every new CVE and every
botnet that switches targets. Any printed list of "the most attacked ports" is a
photograph, and its value lies in explaining *why* those ports, not in
memorising the order.
</FieldNote>

## Changing the number is not a defense

It is the most repeated piece of advice and the most misunderstood: "move SSH
from 22 to 2222".

<Callout type="trap" label="Why that barely helps">
Two reasons, and the second one settles the argument:

1. **Sweeping every port of one target takes seconds.** If somebody has already
   decided to attack you specifically, the number does not slow them down
   meaningfully.
2. **The service introduces itself.** As soon as the connection opens, the SSH
   server sends a line that literally reads `SSH-2.0-OpenSSH_9.6`. The attacker
   does not have to infer from the port — he asks and receives the name and the
   version. This is called *banner grabbing*, and it holds for nearly every
   service.

There is a gain, and it is a different one: **less log noise**. Moving the
service off the standard port stops the large-scale automated sweeps, and
whatever is left in the log stands a chance of being somebody actually looking
at you. That is valuable for *detection*. Just do not mistake being quiet for
being protected.
</Callout>

## Three different things called "port"

Anyone working with containers trips over this weekly, and the lesson
[Networking, internal DNS and the port that bypasses your firewall](/en/lessons/networking-and-dns/)
unpacks it from the builder's side. From the attacker's side, the summary is:

- **`EXPOSE 8080`** in a Dockerfile — documentation. It opens nothing.
- **The port the process listens on** — this is the one that truly exists,
  inside the container.
- **`ports:` in Compose** — publishing: it brings the container's port onto a
  host interface. **It is the only one of the three that grows your attack
  surface.**

<Callout type="danger" label="The prefix that decides everything">
```yaml
ports:
  - "8080:8080"              # listens on 0.0.0.0 — the entire internet
  - "127.0.0.1:8080:8080"    # the local host only
```

And do not count on the firewall: Docker writes NAT rules into a chain evaluated
**before** UFW's or firewalld's rules. `ufw status` says "deny incoming" and the
port is open anyway, because the firewall is never consulted.

Every `ports:` in this stack carries the `127.0.0.1:` prefix, and step 1 of
`make attack-lab` fails if any of them loses it.
</Callout>

## This stack's surface, measured

Six services running. One reaches the host.

<RunIt title="Look at your own machine's ports, and at this stack's">

```bash
# 1. Everything listening on YOUR host, with the process owning each socket.
#    You will probably find something you forgot was running.
ss -ltnp

# 2. What the stack publishes. Look at the prefix: 127.0.0.1 is loopback,
#    0.0.0.0 is the entire internet.
make up
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml ps

# 3. The three answers, live. From inside the proxy, against two neighbours:
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml \
  exec edge sh -c 'nc -z -w2 api 8080 && echo "api:8080 OPEN"'
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml \
  exec edge sh -c 'nc -z -w2 db 5432 || echo "db:5432 unreachable — not even a route"'
```

</RunIt>

<LabExercise check="1/11 — host port sweep" file="stack/compose.yaml">
Without looking at `compose.yaml`: the stack has six services and every one of
them listens on some port. How many of those ports can an attacker on the
internet reach, and which command answers that with certainty?


<Fragment slot="hint">
The question is not how many ports exist — it is how many were **published**,
and on which interface. Two different things.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">
**None.** The stack's only publication is `127.0.0.1:8080`, which is loopback:
reachable only from inside the host itself. Everything else talks over Docker's
internal network, on the container's port, with no publishing at all.

```bash
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml ps --format json \
  | grep -o '"URL":"[^"]*"' | sort -u
```

If `0.0.0.0` shows up on any line, that port is published to the world. Step 1
of `make attack-lab` runs exactly this check and fails when the prefix
disappears from any service.
</Fragment>

## What sticks

- A port is a **number in a system table**, not a place. With no process
  listening, there is no port.
- The attacker sweeps **one port across millions of addresses**, not the other
  way round. You were not chosen; you were on the list.
- The three answers — open, closed, filtered — are the map. The absence of an
  answer informs too.
- Changing the number reduces **noise**, not risk: the service introduces itself
  on the first line of the conversation.
- The defense that works is the port **not existing from the outside**. Which is
  what the next lesson shows holding for 22, 23 and 3389.
