## The clause, and what it changed

On **10 August 2023** HashiCorp announced that Terraform would move from MPL 2.0
to **BUSL 1.1**. The first release published under the new licence was **1.6.0,
on 4 October 2023**.

<Term word="BUSL" alt="BUSL — Business Source License">
A *source-available* licence: the code is there to read and modify, and there is
a usage restriction — typically, not competing with whoever publishes it.

It is not open source by the OSI definition, and the difference is not
philosophical: it changes who may build a product on top, who may offer the
managed service, and what your company's legal department will answer.

Each version usually becomes open source after a delay (four years, in this
case), which helps little for anyone who needs the current release.
</Term>

The community's answer was a fork: **OpenTF**, which became **OpenTofu** on
20 September 2023 when the Linux Foundation accepted it. It remains under
MPL 2.0 and OSI approved. In **2025**, IBM closed its acquisition of HashiCorp.

<Callout type="why" label="Why this is not community gossip">
Terraform's `LICENSE` scopes BUSL to "Terraform Version 1.6.0 or later". Since
no current release predates that, **every version you would download today**
falls under it.

For most uses — provisioning your own infrastructure — that changes nothing in
practice. But it is a sentence somebody will have to write on a form, and it is
better to know it beforehand.
</Callout>

## What actually diverges

| | Terraform | OpenTofu |
|---|---|---|
| licence | BUSL 1.1 (since 1.6.0) | MPL 2.0, OSI approved |
| governance | IBM / HashiCorp | Linux Foundation |
| language | HCL | HCL — the same |
| providers | same | same, with a registry of its own |
| state encryption | no, in the open CLI | **yes**, since 1.7 |
| keeping secrets out of state | `ephemeral`, write-only | — |

The first two rows are politics. The fifth and sixth are technical and opposite:
**OpenTofu encrypts the file; Terraform keeps the secret from reaching it.**
Lesson 7 measures the first and cites the second.

<Callout type="trap" label="The job post says 'Terraform' and that changes nothing">
The language is the same, the providers are the same, the commands are the same.
What you learned in this track works in a Terraform **without translation** —
`tofu plan` and `terraform plan` take the same flags and produce the same output.

Teaching the minority tool to make a point of taste would be a disservice. That
is not the case here: the choice has a measured technical consequence.
</Callout>

## What this module does NOT prove

The most important part of the lesson, and the reason it exists. Module 4
provisions containers on a local daemon. That covers state, graph, plan, drift,
provider and pinning — and does **not** cover:

- **cloud primitives**: VPC, subnet, security group, IAM. That is most of the
  HCL written in the industry, and none of it appears here;
- **remote state with real locking**: the backend is a local file. Concurrency
  and durability were never exercised;
- **cost**: there is no invoice, so there is no most-expensive lesson of all —
  the one about a `destroy` in the wrong directory;
- **published modules and team workspaces**: those are team practices, and a
  team of one does not exercise them.

<FieldNote

>
The 1.12 series brought OCI registry support for providers and modules, native
S3 state locking (with no DynamoDB table needed just for that) and experimental
OpenTelemetry tracing. Native S3 locking is the most practical of the three: it
removes an entire resource from the design of any remote backend on AWS.
</FieldNote>

<Callout type="danger" label="The `destroy` nobody forgets">
In the cloud, `tofu destroy` in the wrong directory deletes real infrastructure,
and the `-auto-approve` that is convenient in a lab is what removes the last
question before it.

The known defences — `prevent_destroy` in `lifecycle`, separate state per
environment, read-only credentials by default — are exactly the ones a local
module cannot exercise, because here deleting everything costs 24 seconds to
rebuild.
</Callout>

<LabExercise check="zero containers, redes e volumes com o rótulo do módulo" file="tools/scripts/iac-verify.sh">

The gate runs `tofu destroy` and then checks that nothing is left carrying the
`infra-knowlogy.module=iac` label. But it does **not** check the images — and
they stay on the host on purpose.

Question: why do the images stay, and what does that teach about `destroy`?


<Fragment slot="hint">
Look for `keep_locally` in `iac/images.tf` and think about who else uses those
digests.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

Because the base images are **shared**. The `postgres:17-alpine` at that digest
is the same one `compose.yaml` and the kind cluster use; deleting it during
module 4's `destroy` would force the other two to download everything again.

```hcl
resource "docker_image" "base" {
  # ...
  keep_locally = true
}
```

The general lesson is that **`destroy` deletes what state lists, and state lists
what you declared** — including things somebody else also uses. With a cloud
provider that has a name and a bill: a subnet declared in two different states is
destroyed by whichever runs first, and the second finds out afterwards.

The right boundary is not technical but one of ownership: every resource should
belong to **one** state, and a shared resource enters by reference (`data`),
never by declaration.

</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- Terraform is BUSL since 1.6.0; OpenTofu is the MPL 2.0 fork at the Linux
  Foundation.
- The language is the same: what you learned here works in either.
- The technical divergence that matters: state encryption on one side,
  `ephemeral` and write-only on the other.
- This module proves no cloud, no real locking, no cost and no team practice —
  and saying so is part of teaching.
- `destroy` deletes what state lists. A shared resource enters by reference, not
  by declaration.
