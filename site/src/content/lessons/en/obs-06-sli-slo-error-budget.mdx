Three acronyms, and confusing them is expensive.

| acronym | what it is | example in this stack |
|---|---|---|
| **SLI** | the **measurement** | ratio of user requests without 5xx |
| **SLO** | the **target** for that measurement | 99.5% over 30 days |
| **error budget** | what the target leaves over | 0.5% of requests may fail |

<ErrorBudget />

## The denominator is where almost every SLI is born wrong

This is the measured part, and it was a surprise. With the stack idle and only
monitoring running, the requests the api serves break down like this:

```
/healthz    1714
/readyz     1708
/metrics    1142
real user traffic                 132
```

**More than twenty infrastructure requests for every user request** — and that
ratio swings with load. The number that does **not** swing is the floor:
healthchecks every 10 s on two routes give 12 per minute, and scrapes every
15 s add 4 more. **Sixteen requests per minute, with zero users, forever.**

Any service receiving fewer than sixteen requests per minute has its SLI
denominator dominated by its own monitoring. An SLI computed
over *all* requests would have a denominator twenty-five times larger than the
numerator that matters — and a **total** outage would show up as 96% success.

<Callout type="danger" label="The alert that never fires">
With a 99.5% SLO and a denominator diluted like that, the service would have to
be failing for hours before the SLI crossed the threshold. In practice: the
dashboard stays green for the entire incident.

That is why this stack's `slo.yml` begins with an exclusion rather than with a
metric:

```promql
sum(rate(api_http_requests_total{route!~"/healthz|/readyz|/metrics"}[5m]))
```

It is not an implementation detail. It is the difference between measuring the
user and measuring yourself.
</Callout>

## And 4xx stays out of the budget

```promql
sum(rate(api_http_requests_total{route!~"…", status=~"5.."}[5m]))
```

Only `5xx` counts as an error. A `404` is the client asking for something that
does not exist — putting 4xx in the budget makes the user's typo spend your
team's budget.

<Callout type="trap" label="The exception that proves the rule">
There are 4xx that **are** your fault: a `429` from a badly calibrated rate
limit, a `400` caused by an API contract you broke.

The practical rule is not the number, it is the question: **was this error
caused by something I control?** If yes, it counts. If it is the client asking
wrongly, it does not — and the decision has to be written down, because it will
be challenged during the first incident.
</Callout>

<Term word="recording rule" alt="recording rule" open>
A PromQL expression Prometheus evaluates periodically and **stores as a new
series**, under its own name.

They exist for two reasons. The first is performance: the SLI expression crosses
two aggregations over a window, and recomputing it in every dashboard and every
alert is waste. The second matters more — it gives the concept a **name**.

`api:sli_erro:ratio5m` reads well in an alert, on a dashboard and in a
conversation; the raw expression does not. The `level:metric:operation`
convention comes from the Prometheus documentation and exists so the name itself
says the value is derived, not scraped.
</Term>

## The budget as a decision, not a number

An SLO with no written consequence is one more number on a dashboard. What makes
it useful is the **error budget policy** — an agreement made before the
incident:

- **budget remaining** → risk is allowed: deploys, migrations, experiments;
- **budget exhausted** → freeze changes until it recovers.

<Callout type="why" label="Why this settles an argument instead of starting one">
The tension between shipping and stability is permanent, and is usually settled
by whoever speaks loudest or has the most seniority in the room.

The error budget replaces that with a number both sides agreed on **beforehand**.
It does not remove the conflict — it moves it to a moment when nobody is under
pressure, which is when good decisions happen.

And it cuts both ways: a remaining budget is an argument **in favour** of taking
more risk. A 100% SLO is not ambition, it is a refusal to ship anything.
</Callout>

<LabExercise check="o SLI de disponibilidade tem valor" file="stack/services/observability/rules/slo.yml">

`slo.yml` ends each ratio with `or vector(0)`. Without it, the expression would
just be the division.

Question: what is `or vector(0)` fixing, and why does that matter more in an
alert than on a dashboard?

<Fragment slot="hint">
Think about what the division returns when there is no traffic at all.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

With no traffic, numerator and denominator are both zero. `0/0` in PromQL is not
an error: it is **NaN**, and a series holding NaN disappears from the result.

On a dashboard that shows up as a gap in the graph — ugly and harmless.

In an alert it is serious: the alert expression compares the SLI against a
threshold, and a comparison with an **absent** series is neither true nor false
— it simply produces no result. The alert does not fire and does not refuse. It
stops existing, silently.

```promql
(api:erros_usuario:rate5m / api:trafego_usuario:rate5m) or vector(0)
```

`or vector(0)` guarantees a value always exists: zero errors when there is no
traffic, which is the correct reading.

The cruel detail is when this bites: at night, with little traffic, the
denominator drops to zero in some windows. That is exactly the hour when nobody
is watching the dashboard and the alert is the only defence.

The general lesson applies to every alert: **an expression that can return empty
is an alert that can fail to exist.** Test with the series absent, not only with
the series wrong.

</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- SLI is the measurement, SLO is the target, the budget is what the target
  leaves over.
- The **denominator** decides everything, and the infrastructure floor is
  constant: 16 requests per minute with zero users, measured and derivable from
  the intervals.
- 4xx stays out of the budget — except when the cause is yours, and that must be
  written down.
- A recording rule gives the concept a **name**, on top of saving computation.
- With no consequence policy, an SLO is one more number on a dashboard.
- `or vector(0)`: an expression that can return empty is an alert that can fail
  to exist.
