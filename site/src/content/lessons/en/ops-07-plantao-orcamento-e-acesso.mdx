This lesson is **cited**, like the previous one: on-call, invoices and access
policy are not measurable on a laptop. There is one exception, and it is at the
end.

## Sleep

<Term word="Alert fatigue" alt="alert fatigue">
The state in which an on-call person stops reacting to alerts because most of
them required no action.

It is not indiscipline: it is adaptation. An alert that fires and asks for
nothing **trains** whoever receives it to ignore it, and the training does not
distinguish that alert from the others. The cost shows up in the next incident,
when the alert that mattered arrives inside the noise.

The remedy is unpopular because it means **deleting alerts** — and an existing
alert always looks useful to whoever wrote it.
</Term>

Chapter 11 of the SRE Book gives a number that works as a ruler:

> **At most 2 incidents per 12-hour shift.**

The justification is arithmetic, not philosophical: responding to an incident
with analysis, remediation and follow-up takes **about 6 hours** on average. Two
incidents already fill the shift. Beyond that, either the analysis does not
happen or the next shift inherits the debt.

The book adds the expectation that bothers people most: the median number of
pages per shift should be **zero**.

This stack already has the piece that makes that possible, and it lives in
another track: the [burn-rate alert](/en/lessons/burn-rate-alerting) fires on
**error budget being consumed too fast**, not on a threshold being crossed. It is
the difference between "latency went above 300 ms" — which happens every Tuesday
— and "we are spending the month in two days".

## Money

<Term word="FinOps" alt="FinOps">
The practice of treating infrastructure cost as an engineering decision, with the
same cycles as other decisions: measure, allocate, optimise.

The FinOps Foundation organises that into three phases — **Inform**,
**Optimize**, **Operate** — and four domains, the first of which is
*understanding usage and cost*. The order matters: nearly every attempt to
optimise before allocating cost ends up cutting whatever was cheap.
</Term>

The structural problem is **delay and distance**:

- the architecture decision happens today;
- the invoice arrives in thirty or sixty days;
- and whoever picks the instance type **never sees the invoice**.

The consequence is that cost gets no course correction, unlike latency or error
rate, which come back in minutes.

The metric the FinOps Foundation pushes is not total cost: it is **cost per unit
of business** — per order, per active user, per gigabyte served. A total cost
that doubles while traffic triples is good news, and the absolute number cannot
say so.

On Kubernetes, **OpenCost** (CNCF incubating) does that allocation per container,
summing CPU, memory, volumes and load balancers. It is the piece that translates
`requests` and `limits` — which this repository measures — into currency.

## Access

Here there is a **measured** bridge, and it is the only part of this lesson that
is not cited.

Module 3 proves, by command, the asymmetry of signing artefacts in two worlds:

- on **GitHub Actions** the signature is *keyless*: the runner presents a
  very short-lived OIDC token, the certificate service validates it and issues an
  ephemeral certificate. **No key is stored anywhere.**
- on **self-hosted Jenkins** the same signature costs a managed key — which
  exists, must be rotated, and can leak.

<Tradeoff
  a="Long-lived secret"
  b="OIDC federation"
  whenA="When the consumer cannot speak OIDC — a legacy system, a vendor accepting only an API key, a script running outside any platform with an identity."
  whenB="Whenever workload identity is available. You trade a stored secret for verifiable trust, and what leaks stops being worth anything within minutes."
>
  <Fragment slot="a">
    Works anywhere and is trivial to configure. In exchange: it exists until
    somebody rotates it, and a leak is valid until somebody notices.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="b">
    The token lives for minutes and is bound to the repository, the branch and the
    workflow. In exchange: it needs a trust relationship configured on both sides,
    and misconfiguration is harder to diagnose.
  </Fragment>
</Tradeoff>

The audit question that sums the subject up: **how many long-lived secrets exist,
and who may use them?** It is countable — and almost never counted.

The chapter states a limit of **2 incidents per 12-hour shift**, derived from an
estimate of ~6 hours per incident once analysis and follow-up are included. And
it records that pages should be "very flat over time, with a likely median value
of 0", warning that a component paging daily will eventually break some other
way. These are numbers from an organisation with large teams and wide rotations;
on a team of five the same arithmetic gives a different answer. Not measured
here: this stack has no on-call.
</FieldNote>

<LabExercise
  question="You inherited an on-call rotation with 30 configured alerts and nobody remembers why they exist. Where do you start?"
  check="postgres: a extensão existe no banco"
>
  <Fragment slot="hint">
    Do not start with the alerts. Start with the history of what they caused.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">

For each alert over the last 90 days, answer two questions:

1. **How many times did it fire?**
2. **How many times did anyone do something because of it?**

The ratio between the two is what matters. An alert with a **high fire rate and a
low action rate** is not an alert: it is noise with interrupt priority, and it is
actively training the team to ignore the whole queue.

Three destinations, and none of them is "leave as is":

- **delete** — nobody acted on it in 90 days;
- **demote to a ticket** — it deserves attention, it does not deserve waking
  somebody (this stack's `slo.yml` uses exactly that severity);
- **rewrite as burn rate** — the symptom is real, but the fixed threshold fires
  on normal variation.

The deciding test: **if this alert fires at 3 a.m., is there an action the
on-call person can take right now?** If the answer is "look at it and see whether
it gets worse", it is a ticket.

  </Fragment>
</LabExercise>
