## Burn rate is speed, not quantity

<Term word="burn rate" alt="burn rate" open>
The speed at which the error budget is being spent, in multiples of the pace
that would exhaust it exactly at the end of the window.

With a 99.5% SLO over 30 days, **burn rate 1** means spending 0.5% of requests
across the whole 30 days — exactly the agreed limit. **Burn rate 14.4** means
spending at the speed that empties the budget in a little over two days.

The number 14.4 is not arbitrary: it is `(30 days / 1 hour) × 2%`. It is the
rate that consumes 2% of the budget in one hour, and it is the SRE Workbook's
recipe.
</Term>

The change of perspective is this: the alert does not ask "is the error rate
high?", it asks "**at this speed, when does the budget run out?**". A 0.6% rate
is catastrophic for a 99.9% SLO and irrelevant for a 95% one — and only burn
rate captures that.

## The AND between windows

```yaml
expr: >
  api:sli_erro:ratio1h > (14.4 * 0.005)
  and
  api:sli_erro:ratio5m > (14.4 * 0.005)
for: 2m
```

<Callout type="why" label="Why two windows, and never one">
**Long window only (1h):** a five-minute spike keeps the one-hour average above
the threshold for *a full hour*. The alert fires correctly and then stays stuck,
long after the problem is gone. Whoever is on call learns to ignore it.

**Short window only (5m):** any hiccup becomes a page. Three days of that and
nobody reads alerts any more.

**Both with `and`:** the long one says the problem is *sustained*, the short one
says it is *still happening*. Fires fast and clears fast.
</Callout>

## Measured: the gate stops the database and the alert fires

This is not a documentation example. While this track was being written,
Postgres was deliberately stopped and user traffic kept flowing:

```
   t     ratio5m   ratio1h   14.4x threshold   alerts
     0s   1.0000    1.0000   0.0720        OrcamentoDeErroQueimandoRapido=firing,
                                           OrcamentoDeErroQueimandoMedio=pending,
                                           OrcamentoDeErroEmRitmoDeTicket=pending
```

The SLI went to 1.0 — **100% errors** — and the three alerts reacted at
different speeds, which is exactly the design: the 14.4× one already `firing`,
the slower ones still accumulating.

<Callout type="trap" label="And it does not clear when you fix it">
With the database back and traffic answering 201, the alert **kept firing**.

That is not a defect. The five-minute window still contains the errors; it has
to drain. The alert clears when the short window empties, not when you fix
things — and that is precisely why the short window exists: without it, the wait
would be the one-hour window.

Worth knowing before the incident. An alert that stays red after the fix makes
half the room believe the problem came back.
</Callout>

## The alert that wakes nobody

```yaml
- alert: OrcamentoDeErroEmRitmoDeTicket
  labels:
    severity: ticket
```

The third alert in `slo.yml` has severity `ticket`, not `page`. It exists
because **most of the budget is spent exactly like that**: slowly, with no
incident at all, over weeks — and no paging alert ever fires.

<Tradeoff
  a="Alerting on symptoms (CPU, disk, raw latency)"
  b="Alerting on the budget's burn rate"
  whenA="For conditions that are causes rather than consequences, and where the action is obvious: disk at 95%, a certificate expiring in 7 days. Nobody needs an SLO to know what to do about those."
  whenB="For anything the user feels. A symptom does not imply impact — CPU at 100% with fast responses is not an incident, and CPU at 30% with timeouts is."

<LabExercise check="toda regra de SLO avalia sem erro" file="stack/services/observability/rules/slo.yml">

The three alerts in `slo.yml` have different `for:` values: `2m`, `15m` and
`1h`.

Question: why does the fastest one have the shortest `for:`, and what would
happen if all of them had `for: 0s`?

<Fragment slot="hint">
`for:` is how long the condition must stay true BEFORE the alert becomes
`firing`.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

Because `for:` is a third anti-noise layer, on top of the two windows — and it
has to be proportional to the urgency.

The 14.4× alert already has a five-minute short window in the `and`; demanding
two more minutes of confirmation is cheap and still reacts in ~7 minutes. The
ticket-pace one looks at 6 hours: waiting another hour changes nothing, and it
avoids opening a ticket over a fluctuation.

With `for: 0s` on all of them, the alert fires on the first evaluation cycle
where the condition holds — including a window where the denominator dropped to
nearly zero and two errors became 100%. At night, with little traffic, that
happens.

```promql
# two requests, both failing, and the SLI goes to 1.0
api:sli_erro:ratio5m
```

Lesson 6's `or vector(0)` protects against **zero** traffic. It does not protect
against **low** traffic — and that is where `for:` comes in.

The rule that follows: the three layers do different things. The long window
filters what is not sustained, the short one filters what has already passed,
and `for:` filters what was a sampling artifact. Removing any one has a cost,
and removing all three is the alert nobody can live with.

</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- Burn rate is **speed** of spending, not error rate — and it is what makes the
  threshold comparable across services with different SLOs.
- 14.4× comes from `(30d / 1h) × 2%`. The recipe's numbers are derived, not
  magic.
- Two windows with `and`: the long one says it is sustained, the short one says
  it is still happening.
- Measured: database stopped → SLI at 1.0 → alert `firing`. And it **does not
  clear on the fix**, it clears when the window drains.
- Not every alert wakes somebody: the slow-pace one becomes a ticket, and that
  is how most of the budget disappears.
- `firing` is not a notification. The Alertmanager is what notifies, and it now
  exists — with routing, grouping and silencing proven by the gate.
