There are four types, and the confusion concentrates in one of them.

| type | what it is | example in this stack |
|---|---|---|
| **counter** | a number that only grows (or resets on restart) | `api_http_requests_total` |
| **gauge** | a number that goes up and down | `container_memory_working_set_bytes` |
| **histogram** | cumulative counters per bucket | `api_http_request_duration_seconds` |
| **summary** | quantiles computed **on the client** | — |

## A counter is not read, it is derived

A counter on its own almost never answers anything.
`api_http_requests_total` reads 4,688 — is that a lot? It depends on how long it
has been counting, and it resets every time the process restarts.

```promql
rate(api_http_requests_total[5m])
```

<Callout type="why" label="What `rate()` does besides dividing">
It computes the per-second slope inside the window **and handles resets**: when
the value drops — because the process restarted — `rate()` understands there was
a restart instead of recording a giant negative rate.

That is why the naming convention matters. Applying `rate()` to a gauge produces
a number, and the number is garbage: a gauge that falls did not restart, it just
went down.
</Callout>

<Term word="range window" alt="range vector" open>
The `[5m]` is not a filter: it changes the **type** of what the expression
returns.

Without brackets, the expression returns one value per series — the most recent
one. With brackets, it returns the **series of samples** in that window, and
that is what functions like `rate()` and `increase()` require in order to exist.

The practical rule that avoids the most common mistake: the window must contain
at least **two** scrapes. With `scrape_interval: 15s`, `[30s]` is the honest
minimum — `[10s]` returns empty, and empty fires no alert.
</Term>

## The histogram, and what it actually stores

<HistogramBuckets />

The application does **not** send durations. It sends a handful of counters:
`..._bucket{le="0.005"}`, `le="0.01"`, and so on, each counting how many
requests fell below that bound.

```promql
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(api_http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[10m])))
```

<Callout type="danger" label="There is no such thing as an average percentile">
If you have ten servers, each with its own p99, the average of the ten **is
not** the p99 of the whole. It is not a poor approximation: it is a number that
corresponds to no request at all.

The correct way is to sum the ten servers' **buckets** and compute the
percentile at the end — which is exactly what the `sum by (le)` in the
expression above does, and the reason it is there.

It is also the practical difference between `histogram` and `summary`: a summary
computes the quantile inside the process, and a client-side quantile **cannot be
aggregated afterwards**. That is why this stack uses histograms.
</Callout>

## The precision is the bucket's width

The p99 is interpolated inside the bucket where the 99th percentile falls. If
the buckets go from `0.05` straight to `0.1`, every p99 in that range is a
linear guess between 50 ms and 100 ms.

The Go client's `DefBuckets` are a generic default, designed for web requests in
the tens-to-hundreds of milliseconds. Measured in this stack under normal
operation: **p50 of 2.8 ms**, well under the first bucket — meaning almost every
request lands in the `le="0.005"` bucket, where resolution is coarse.

<Callout type="trap" label="A histogram carries the incident for the whole window">
While this track was being written, the gate deliberately stopped Postgres to
prove the burn-rate alert. In the measurements taken right after, the p99 rose
to **4.7 seconds** — and stayed there for thirty minutes, because the `[30m]`
window still contained the outage's requests.

It is not a measurement error: it is what the window means. Anyone reading
percentiles during an incident needs to know how much history they are looking
at, or they will believe the problem persists after it is fixed.
</Callout>

`metrics.go` declares `api_cache_hits_total` and `api_cache_misses_total` as two
separate counters.

Question: why not a single one, with a `result="hit"|"miss"` label?


<Fragment slot="hint">
Think about how you would write the cache hit ratio in PromQL under each design.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">

Both designs work, and the single-label one is **better** — it is the convention
Prometheus itself recommends.

With a label, the hit ratio comes out directly:

```promql
rate(api_cache_requests_total{result="hit"}[5m])
  / ignoring(result) sum without (result) (rate(api_cache_requests_total[5m]))
```

With two names, you have to write both names in every expression, and there is
no way to sum "all cache lookups" without listing them by hand. Adding a third
outcome — `error`, say — means touching every dashboard.

The general rule: **what varies becomes a label, what is a different thing
becomes a name.** `hit` and `miss` are the same event with different outcomes;
`requests` and `duration` are different things.

The limit of that rule is lesson 4: labels are cheap until they are not. Two
fixed values, no problem; a `user_id`, and you take Prometheus down.

</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- A counter is not read, it is derived — and `rate()` handles process resets.
- `[5m]` changes the **type** of the result, and must contain at least two
  scrapes.
- A histogram stores **cumulative counters per bucket**, not durations.
- `histogram_quantile` interpolates: the precision is the bucket's width.
- **Averaging p99 values means nothing.** Sum the buckets, take the quantile at
  the end.
- A summary computes quantiles on the client, and therefore cannot be aggregated
  across instances.
