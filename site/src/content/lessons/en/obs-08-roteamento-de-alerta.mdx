The [previous lesson](/en/lessons/burn-rate-alerting) ended with a red box
admitting there was no Alertmanager here: Prometheus marked `firing` and nobody
was notified.

This lesson closes that. And the first thing to undo is the confusion of roles.

## Who does what

<AlertPipeline />

| | decides |
|---|---|
| **Prometheus** | **WHEN** there is a problem — evaluates the rule, waits out the `for:`, marks `firing` |
| **Alertmanager** | **TO WHOM, WHEN and WHETHER** to tell |

Prometheus knows nothing about on-call shifts, nor that two alerts tell the same
story. It evaluates expressions. Everything after that is a different program —
and it is the half that decides whether the team reads the channel or muted it.

<Callout type="warn" title="The block that is missing and never complains">
```yaml
alerting:
  alertmanagers:
    - static_configs:
        - targets: [alertmanager:9093]
```

Without this in `prometheus.yml`, Prometheus evaluates, marks `firing` in its own
UI, and **sends it nowhere**. There is no error, no warning: the alerts page turns
red and the rest of the world never finds out.

To check: `/api/v1/alertmanagers` returns `activeAlertmanagers`. An empty list
means the alert does not leave.
</Callout>

## The four operations

<Term word="Grouping" alt="grouping">
```yaml
group_by: [alertname, severity]
group_wait: 30s
group_interval: 5m
repeat_interval: 4h
```

Without grouping, a downed database generates **one notification per series** —
and the on-call person receives thirty messages saying the same thing. It is the
fastest way to train somebody to mute the channel.

The three timers do different things, and confusing them is common:

- **`group_wait`** — waits before the FIRST message of a new group, so sibling
  alerts arrive together;
- **`group_interval`** — waits before reporting NEW alerts in a group that
  already notified;
- **`repeat_interval`** — before repeating the SAME message. Too low is spam;
  too high makes a long incident vanish from the channel.
</Term>

**Routing.** This stack's SLO rules already emitted `severity: page` and
`severity: ticket`, and until now the distinction did nothing:

```yaml
route:
  receiver: plantao
  routes:
    - matchers: [severity="ticket"]
      receiver: fila
      group_wait: 5m
      repeat_interval: 24h
```

That separation is what makes the SRE Book's expectation of **a median of zero
pages per shift** possible. The 1× burn rate — the budget will run out at the end
of the month — is Tuesday work, not 3 a.m. work.

**Inhibition.** When the fast burn rate fires, the medium one is firing too: they
measure the same burn over different windows.

```yaml
inhibit_rules:
  - source_matchers: [alertname="OrcamentoDeErroQueimandoRapido"]
    target_matchers: [alertname="OrcamentoDeErroQueimandoMedio"]
    equal: [service]
```

The `equal:` is what stops inhibition leaking across services — without it, a
problem in the api would silence the worker's medium alert.

**Silencing.** Whoever is resolving an incident needs to stop being told about it
**without losing the alert for next time**. A silence does not delete the rule:
the alert stays active and moves to the `suppressed` state.

```console
$ curl -s :9093/api/v2/alerts | jq '.[] | {alertname:.labels.alertname, state:.status.state}'
{"alertname": "PortaoEntregaPage…", "state": "suppressed"}
```

## Proving it left, from two sides

The gate measures delivery in two ways on purpose:

```console
alertmanager_notifications_total{integration="webhook"}         1 → 2
alertmanager_notifications_failed_total{integration="webhook"}  0
```

```json
{"request":{"method":"POST","uri":"/plantao"},"status":200}
```

The counter says Alertmanager **tried and succeeded**. The receiver's access log
says it **arrived**.

<Callout type="note" title="Why both, and not one">
The counter alone would accept a receiver that answers `200` and discards the
message — which is exactly what a misconfigured webhook does.

The receiver's log alone would not distinguish "Alertmanager did not send" from
"it sent and I did not log it". Measuring both ends is what closes the claim.
</Callout>

<Tradeoff
  a="Webhook to your own receiver"
  b="Direct integration (Slack, PagerDuty)"
  whenA="When the alert needs PROCESSING before it becomes a message — enriching with context, opening a ticket, correlating. And when you do not want a third-party credential in the repository, which is the case here."
  whenB="When the destination is a person and the message is the message. Your own webhook in the middle is one more component to maintain, and it sits between the incident and whoever responds."
>
  <Fragment slot="a">
    No third-party secret, and the format is yours. In exchange: one more service
    that can fall over — and it falls over precisely during the incident.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="b">
    Fewer pieces, and Alertmanager retries on its own when delivery fails. In
    exchange: a long-lived credential in the repository, with everything the
    [access lesson](/en/lessons/on-call-cost-and-access) says about that.
  </Fragment>
</Tradeoff>

## Two things broke before working

**Caddy's access log needs the `log` directive INSIDE the site block.** In the
global block it configures only Caddy's own logger — the startup and TLS
messages. The receiver's first version answered `200` and logged nothing: the
Alertmanager's counter already said the notification had left, and the receiver
had no proof of having received it.

**The delivery check only passed once.** With `group_by` on `alertname` and a
`repeat_interval` of 4 hours, the gate's second run on the same day generated no
new notification — and the check failed for working correctly. The synthetic
alert now gets a unique name per run.

<FieldNote
  source="Google SRE Workbook — Alerting on SLOs"
  url="https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/"
  accessed="2026-09-21"
>
The chapter argues that most of an alerting system's value lies in **not**
alerting: every page requiring no action trains its recipient to ignore the ones
that do, and that training does not distinguish between them. The recommendation
is to keep only alerts with a clear, immediate action and push the rest to
tickets — which is the split this stack's `route:` implements. Not measured here:
its effectiveness is measured across a team over months, not in a gate.
</FieldNote>

<LabExercise
  question="An alert has been firing in Prometheus for twenty minutes and nobody received anything. What are the three places to look, in order?"
  check="o prometheus conhece o alertmanager"
>
  <Fragment slot="hint">
    The path has three hops, and each has its own way of failing silently.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">

**1. Does Prometheus know where to send?**

```bash
curl -s :9090/api/v1/alertmanagers | jq '.data.activeAlertmanagers'
```

An empty list = the `alerting:` block is missing. It is the quietest of the three
failures: nothing errors, and the alert simply does not travel.

**2. Did the alert REACH the Alertmanager?**

```bash
curl -s :9093/api/v2/alerts | jq '.[] | {a:.labels.alertname, s:.status.state}'
```

If the state is `suppressed`, a silence or an inhibition rule is active — and
that is the most confusing cause, because everything else looks right. Check with
`curl -s :9093/api/v2/silences`.

**3. Did it LEAVE?**

```bash
curl -s :9093/metrics | grep -E '^alertmanager_notifications_(total|failed_total)\{integration="webhook"'
```

A rising `failed_total` points at the destination (DNS, credential, network).
Both frozen with the alert active points at timing: the `group_wait` has not
elapsed, or `repeat_interval` already notified and is waiting to repeat.

  </Fragment>
</LabExercise>
