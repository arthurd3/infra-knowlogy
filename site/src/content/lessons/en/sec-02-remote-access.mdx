Look at the poster's eight rows again and notice something: three of them — 22,
23 and 3389 — list nearly the same "common attacks". Brute force, passwords,
social engineering. That is neither coincidence nor a lack of imagination on the
author's part. **They are the same port, in three different decades.**

All three hand over the same thing when somebody gets in: an interactive
session. A terminal or a screen. It is the highest prize on the whole list,
because it is not data leaking — it is the machine starting to take orders from
somebody else.

## The whole family, with the right names

The three terms below get used as synonyms and are not. The difference matters
because each one calls for a different defense.

<Term word="brute force" alt="força bruta" open>
Trying password after password **against the same account**, in the most direct
way possible: a list of common passwords, or every combination of characters.

The classic defense is lockout after N failed attempts — and it works well
precisely because the attack concentrates on one account, which is what trips
the counter.
</Term>

<Term word="credential stuffing" alt="reuse of leaked credentials">
Taking email/password pairs that **have already leaked** from some other service
and trying them on yours. There is no guessing at all: the passwords are real
and belong to real people. The attack works because the same password gets
reused in several places.

Lockout on failed attempts barely helps here: the attacker gets it right on the
first try for each account. The defense is a second factor, which makes the
correct password insufficient on its own.
</Term>

<Term word="password spraying" alt="pulverização de senha">
The inverse of brute force: **one** highly likely password (`Autumn2026!`,
`Company@123`) tried against **thousands of different accounts**.

It is designed to slip under lockout: since each account gets one or two
attempts, no counter ever reaches its limit. Detecting it means looking at the
aggregate — "many distinct accounts failed once each, in the same minute" —
rather than at the individual account. Which is why per-account lockout is
necessary and not sufficient.
</Term>

## 23: the port that was attacked at scale, and how

Telnet is the easiest port to explain because there is no subtlety to it: it
sends the password as readable text and accepts a connection from anyone. What
makes it worth studying is what happened when somebody industrialised the
obvious.

<FieldNote kind="talk" source="Antonakakis et al., USENIX Security 2017" url="https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity17/technical-sessions/presentation/antonakakis" when="2017">
The Mirai botnet study measured the whole thing from the released source code
and from honeypots. The mechanism, in numbers:

- it swept **TCP/23 and TCP/2323**, firing blind SYNs at pseudorandom addresses;
- on finding something that answered, it tried **10 pairs** drawn at random from
  a list of **62 factory credentials**;
- it reached **600,000 infected devices** at its peak, with a **76-minute**
  doubling time early on.

Sixty-two passwords. There was no exploitation of a flaw, no zero day, nothing
resembling security research — just a list of passwords that shipped from the
factory and were never changed, applied at industrial scale.
</FieldNote>

## 22: what "an SSH exploit" actually means

The poster says "exploits of SSH flaws", and the phrase frightens more than it
informs. In practice the overwhelming majority of SSH incidents are credentials,
not software flaws. But the minority exists and is worth knowing by its shape.

<Callout type="why" label="regreSSHion, and why the name carries a pun">
In 2024, CVE-2024-6387 revealed a flaw in `sshd` that granted **code execution
as root, with no authentication**. The mechanism was a signal handler race: if
the client does not authenticate within `LoginGraceTime`, the server fires a
`SIGALRM` handler that calls functions which must not be called from inside a
signal handler.

The pun in the name is the lesson: it was a **regression**. The same flaw had
been fixed in 2006 (CVE-2006-5051) and came back in a 2020 commit, standing in
versions 8.5p1 through 9.7p1 on glibc Linux.

Two things to take away. First: patching is not a task that finishes, because
code moves in both directions. Second: even that flaw required **reaching the
port**. An sshd behind a VPN was not exposed to it.
</Callout>

## 3389: why RDP leads the incident statistics

<FieldNote kind="scale" source="Sophos, Active Adversary Report" url="https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/2025-sophos-active-adversary-report" when="2025 edition, covering 2024 incidents">
Across Sophos's incident response and managed detection cases in 2024,
**compromised credentials were the number one root cause**, at 41% of cases —
the second year running in first place. And RDP showed up in **84%** of cases as
a tool the intruder used after getting in.

The reading that matters: most of the time nobody "broke in" in any technical
sense. Somebody **logged in**, with a credential that worked. Which is why the
defense with the best return is almost never a new product — it is taking the
service off the internet and requiring a second factor.
</FieldNote>

## The real order of effectiveness

Every SSH best-practices list carries the same five items without saying what
each one is worth. They are worth very different things:

| Defense | What it eliminates | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Do not publish** (VPN, bastion) | the whole attack: no reachable service | one more component, which becomes the new target |
| **Keys only, no passwords** | brute force and spraying — nothing to guess | key management: distributing, revoking, protecting |
| **Second factor** | credential stuffing and a lone stolen key | friction in every automation |
| **Attempt rate limiting** | the noise, and it makes naive brute force costly | nothing, and it does not stop a distributed source |
| **Changing the port** | almost nothing (see lesson 1) | nothing, and it creates false confidence |

<Tradeoff
  a="Do not publish: a VPN or bastion in front"
  b="Publish, with keys, MFA and rate limiting"
  whenA="When there is a perimeter to cross and a small, known team. It is the only defense that <b>zeroes</b> the attack instead of making it expensive: what is not reachable is not attackable."
  whenB="When access has to come from anywhere, from people who come and go, or from third-party automation — and maintaining a VPN would cost more attention than it returns in security."
  chose="a"
  verdict="This stack publishes neither SSH nor RDP: neither exists. Getting into a container means <code>docker exec</code>, which talks to the local daemon and opens no port. It is the extreme form of option A — the service <b>does not exist</b>."
/>

<Callout type="trap" label="The defense people forget to measure">
All five rows in the table above are about preventing entry. None of them is
about what happens **after** somebody gets in — and as the Sophos data shows,
walking in with a valid credential is the common case.

Which is why lesson 6 closes the track with layers: whoever gets into a
container in this stack finds a read-only rootfs, no shell in the api image, and
a network with no way out from the database. Preventing entry is the first
layer, not the only one.
</Callout>

<RunIt title="Brute force in miniature, against your own database">

```bash
make up

# The wrong password, from inside the internal network. scram-sha-256 refuses:
docker run --rm -i --network infra-knowlogy_data \
  -e PGPASSWORD=not-this-one postgres:17-alpine \
  psql -h db -U links -d links -c 'SELECT 1'
# FATAL: password authentication failed for user "links"

# The positive control — without it you do not know whether the refusal came
# from the password or from a mistyped hostname:
docker run --rm -i --network infra-knowlogy_data \
  -e PGPASSWORD="$(cat stack/secrets/postgres_password)" postgres:17-alpine \
  psql -h db -U links -d links -c 'SELECT 1'

# Step 4 of the lab runs exactly those two, in that order:
make attack-lab
```

</RunIt>

<LabExercise check="4/11 — wrong password against the database" file="tools/scripts/attack-lab.sh">
Step 4 of `make attack-lab` tries the **wrong** password and then, immediately,
the **right** one. The second test looks redundant — the defense was already
proven by the first. Why is it there?

<Fragment slot="hint">
Ask yourself what else could make the first command fail, besides the password
being wrong.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">
Because without it the gate would be **lying in the stack's own favour**.

A mistyped hostname, a network that does not exist, a stopped container — any of
those makes `psql` fail, and the failure would look like "the defense worked".
The test would pass while authentication might be switched off entirely.

The second command is the **positive control**: it proves the path to the
database works and that the only difference between the two was the password. It
is the same discipline `verify.sh` applies when it separates "the scanner broke"
from "the scanner found a CVE": a check that cannot distinguish the reasons for
a failure is not a check.
</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- 22, 23 and 3389 are one family: whoever gets the credential right wins an
  **interactive session**, the highest prize on the poster.
- Brute force, credential stuffing and spraying are **three different attacks**,
  and per-attempt lockout only hinders the first.
- Mirai exploited no flaw at all: **62 factory passwords** and scale.
- "An SSH exploit" exists and is rare — and even regreSSHion had to **reach the
  port**.
- The defense with the best return is still taking the service off the internet.
  Everything else makes the attack expensive; only that one removes it.
