Lessons 2 and 3 covered two kinds of attack. On 22, 23 and 3389, somebody
**logs in** with a credential. On 80 and 443, somebody exploits a **defect in
your code**.

The three remaining ports are a third kind, and it is the hardest to see coming:
the service has no defect and nobody steals anything. **The attack is the
service working as designed, in somebody else's service.**

## 25: the post office that delivers for anyone

SMTP was born on an internet where all the servers knew each other. On that
internet, "agree to deliver any message that arrives" was hospitality, not a
flaw.

<Term word="open relay" alt="relay aberto" open>
A mail server that agrees to deliver a message **from any sender to any
recipient**, asking for no authentication.

Whoever finds an open relay starts sending spam with your machine, your
bandwidth and — the expensive part — your IP address's reputation. The damage is
not the bandwidth: it is the day your entire domain stops being able to deliver
mail because it landed on a blocklist, and your sales team finds out before you
do.

The correct configuration is simple and is the default on any modern server:
with no authentication, only accept mail **addressed** to one of your domains.
The real risk today is copying a configuration from an old tutorial.
</Term>

<Callout type="why" label="Why 25 and 587 are different ports">
This confuses a lot of people, and the distinction underpins everything else:

- **Port 25** is the conversation between **servers** — one post office handing
  mail to another. It is not for your mail client.
- **Port 587** is **submission** (RFC 6409): you handing your message to YOUR
  server. It always requires authentication.

Separating them is what made it possible to demand a credential from senders
without breaking server-to-server delivery. And it is why cloud providers block
outbound 25 by default: a compromised instance becomes a spam source in minutes,
and almost no legitimate application needs to speak 25 directly.
</Callout>

**Sender spoofing** is the other half. The `From:` field you read in your client
is free text — original SMTP proves nothing about who sent it. The three DNS
records that fix this solve different problems, which is why there are three:

| Record | What it says | What it does not solve |
|---|---|---|
| **SPF** | which servers may send for your domain | it breaks when mail is forwarded |
| **DKIM** | this message was signed by a key of yours | it does not say what to do when the signature fails |
| **DMARC** | ties both to the `From:` the reader sees, and orders mismatches dropped | nothing, if you sit at `p=none` forever |

## 53: the small question that becomes a flood

<Term word="amplification" alt="amplificação">
Making an innocent server send **far more data** than you spent provoking it,
and pointing that data at somebody else.

The mechanism has three parts and all three are required:

1. the protocol is **UDP**, which has no handshake — so the source address can
   be **forged** with nobody verifying it;
2. the **answer is much larger than the question**;
3. there are **many servers** willing to answer strangers.

Remove any one of the three and the attack disappears. Which is why the defense
is not "block UDP" — it is the server no longer answering anyone outside its own
network.
</Term>

<AmplificationLoop />

<FieldNote kind="scale" source="CISA, alert TA14-017A" url="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2014/01/17/udp-based-amplification-attacks" when="2014, revised since">
CISA publishes a table of measured amplification factors by protocol. DNS
appears with a factor between **28 and 54 times**.

The arithmetic that enables is the point: an attacker with 1 Gb/s of upstream
delivers 28 to 54 Gb/s at the victim — and the traffic arrives from legitimate
DNS servers, from addresses that have nothing to do with him. Filtering by
source does not help; the source is innocent.

The full table is worth opening. DNS is not the worst on the list, and some far
less famous protocols amplify a great deal more.
</FieldNote>

**Cache poisoning** is port 53's second attack, and the sneakiest: convincing a
resolver to cache a false answer. From then on, everyone using that resolver
goes to the attacker's server when they type your domain, while your server
stays up and correct. The flaw Dan Kaminsky demonstrated in 2008 made this
practical in seconds; the answers were randomising the query's source port, the
cookies of RFC 7873 and, ultimately, signing answers with DNSSEC.

And there is a third, which returns in lesson 5: **exfiltration over DNS**. When
everything else is blocked, DNS almost always gets out — it is the last port a
firewall closes. The intruder encodes the stolen data in the queried name itself
and reads the result on an authoritative server he controls.

<RunIt title="This stack's resolver serves only its own network — measured">

```bash
make up

# 1. From INSIDE the data network: the resolver answers and knows the neighbours.
docker run --rm --network infra-knowlogy_data alpine nslookup db
# Server: 127.0.0.11 ... Name: db  Address: 172.x.x.x

# 2. From the HOST, that same address is nothing. 127.0.0.11 exists only
#    INSIDE a container's network namespace.
nslookup db 127.0.0.11
# communications error to 127.0.0.11#53: connection refused

# 3. And from ANOTHER network on the same host, the name does not exist.
docker run --rm --network infra-knowlogy_edge alpine nslookup db
# ** server can't find db: NXDOMAIN
```

</RunIt>

The third command is the instructive one. Same resolver address, same machine,
and the name does not resolve — because its scope is the **network**, not the
host. That is exactly the shape the anti-amplification advice recommends: a
resolver serving only its own network amplifies nothing.

## 445: the port that produced a worm

<Term word="worm" alt="verme">
Malicious software that spreads **without anyone clicking anything**. It finds
the next machine on its own, gets in on its own and starts over.

The difference from other malware is time. Phishing needs one distracted person
per machine; a worm needs one reachable vulnerability and grows geometrically.
The window between "a fix exists" and "it no longer matters" closes in hours.
</Term>

<FieldNote kind="postmortem" source="CISA, WannaCry alert" url="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2017/05/12/indicators-associated-wannacry-ransomware" when="May 2017">
The 2017 sequence, by date, because the sequence is the lesson:

- **March**: Microsoft publishes MS17-010, fixing a flaw in SMBv1 that allowed
  code execution with **no credential at all**;
- **May**: WannaCry uses that flaw to spread by itself, machine to machine,
  inside networks.

There were two months of window. The fix existed, was published and was free —
and the damage was global anyway.

Two details that usually get left out of the summary. First: much of the spread
was **inside to inside**. The infection arrived by some other route on some
random machine and, from there, 445 was open between all the others, because the
internal network was flat. Second: no payment undoes this — what mattered was
the network not having 445 running through everything.
</FieldNote>

The other two attacks on 445 exploit no flaw at all and still work today:

- **Pass-the-hash** — Windows authenticates with the password's hash, not the
  password. Whoever reads that hash from the memory of a machine where they had
  local administrator presents it on **another** machine and gets in, never
  learning the password.
- **Authentication relay (NTLM relay)** — the attacker gets your machine to try
  authenticating to him (a link to `\\attacker\x` is enough) and forwards that
  authentication, live, to a third server. He logs in as you without holding
  your password or your hash. Mandatory SMB signing is what breaks this, because
  he cannot re-sign what he forwards.

<Callout type="danger" label="The rule with no exception">
**Port 445 never crosses the network border.** There is no legitimate use case:
file sharing between offices goes over a VPN.

If somebody asks for the exception, the request is the problem — and it is worth
treating it as the signal it is. It is the simplest rule in this whole track and
the one that would have prevented most of WannaCry's spread **between**
organisations.
</Callout>

<LabExercise check="11/11 — exfiltrating from the database" file="stack/compose.yaml">
Exfiltration over DNS exists because DNS almost always has a way out. In this
stack, can the `db` container resolve names? And can it **get out** to the
internet? The two answers differ, and the difference is the defense.


<Fragment slot="hint">
Resolving a name and reaching an address are two things. Look at what
`internal: true` does to the `data` network.
</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="answer">
`db` resolves names **on its own network** — Docker's resolver at `127.0.0.11`
is there and answers for `api`, `cache` and itself. What it does not have is a
**route out**: the `data` network is `internal: true`, which removes the
gateway.

```bash
# resolving a neighbour: works
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml \
  exec db sh -c 'nslookup cache' | tail -3

# reaching the internet: no route
docker compose -f stack/compose.yaml -f stack/compose.prod.yaml \
  exec db sh -c 'nc -z -w3 1.1.1.1 443' || echo "no way out — internal: true"
```

That is step 11 of `make attack-lab`. And it is why exfiltration is hard here:
an attacker with full access to Postgres still has to compromise a second
service — one of those sitting on two networks — just to **send** what he took.
Stealing and carrying away are separate problems, and both are worth defending.
</Fragment>
</LabExercise>

## What sticks

- On these three ports the attacker exploits no defect: he uses the service
  **exactly as it was built to work**.
- Port 25 is the server-to-server conversation; yours is 587, and it demands a
  password. An open relay costs your IP's reputation, not your bandwidth.
- Amplification needs three things at once — UDP, a large answer and a server
  that serves strangers. Remove one and the attack disappears.
- Measured here: this stack's resolver answers only inside its own network. It
  is the correct shape, in miniature.
- 445 never crosses the border. And the border is not the only surface: WannaCry
  spread inside to inside.
